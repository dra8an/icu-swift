## The idea in three sentences

A child looks at the evening sky and waits for a thin moon. On the 29th evening of every month someone must say
whether tomorrow is day 1 or day 30. We follow one real evening, Tuesday 17 February 2026, around the world and watch
the same moon get different answers.

## Working title (pick one, or give another)

1. **The Twenty-Ninth Evening**: how one thin moon starts a month, and why the world does not agree when
2. **One Moon, Many Answers**
3. **Tomorrow, Maybe**

## How it looks

- One night sky on every slide: deep blue above, a warm band at the horizon, stars. Cream words, one warm gold accent.
- The moon is the page number of the story: invisible at the start of the evening, the thinnest thread by the end,
  a clear crescent on the last slide.
- Each place gets its own skyline, cut out in black along the bottom: Singapore's towers, a Malaysian headland, an
  Indonesian shore, the hills of Mecca, the domes of Istanbul, London's roofs, a Moroccan town.
- Each place slide ends the same way, with a small stamp: **Wednesday** or **Thursday**. The audience learns to wait for it.
- Slides dissolve into one another, so the sky never blinks.

## Two choices for you

1. **How much to say on the Saudi evening (slide 14).** The draft says only what was decided and how. The fact sheet
   also holds that an astronomy site says the crescent could not be seen anywhere that evening, and that Oman ruled
   the evening out by calculation. Suggested: keep that off the slide and give it one plain sentence in the notes,
   "astronomers said no eye could have seen it; the court accepted a report that someone did", with no verdict. The
   other way is to leave it out altogether.
2. **London (slide 18).** The draft uses 2026, because it is the same evening as the whole story and both sides are
   read from their own websites. Your own example was an earlier split; 2019 is in the notes. Say if you would rather
   have 2019 on the slide.

## Proposed after approval (2026-11-12; needs the user's yes)

1. **Slide 10, Singapore, a stronger line.** Our engine confirms it: the sun set in Singapore 40 minutes *before* the
   new moon. Proposed words: "The moon was not even new yet. It had set 4 minutes before the sun." The same holds for
   Malaysia (24 minutes before) and Indonesia (10 minutes before) [1b].
2. **Slide 12, Indonesia, a sharper twist.** Muhammadiyah does not use a different rule from Türkiye; it uses the same
   one. That evening the rule came down to one point in Alaska: Muhammadiyah read it as met (Wednesday), Türkiye as not
   met (Thursday). Proposed: keep slide 12 as drafted (government Thursday, Muhammadiyah Wednesday) and put this on
   slide 20, "And the questions change", as its strongest example: "Same rule. Same evening. Two answers." The
   Muhammadiyah article has been read at the source, and our engine puts the Alaska point within 0.04° of the line [2].
3. **Slide 16, Morocco and Pakistan, a truer heading.** Not "only look" but "**witnesses decide**": both use
   calculation to check or advise, and neither lets it decide [2]. The point of the slide stays: for them this was
   day 28.
4. **Slide 9, the map,** is built as a sample from our engine: night where the sun set before the new moon, a line east
   of which the moon set before the sun, the Greenwich-midnight line, and the one zone where Türkiye's numbers were
   met, over the Pacific [1b].
